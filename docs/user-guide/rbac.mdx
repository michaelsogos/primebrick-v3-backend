# Roles and permissions — how access works

Every screen in Primebrick answers one silent question for you: *"is this
person allowed to do this?"* You never see the question being asked — you
simply notice that a colleague can open a page you cannot, or that a button
is greyed out for some users and not for others. This page explains, in
plain terms, how that decision is made and who controls it.

## The idea in one picture

A user never holds a permission directly. Permissions are bundled into
**roles**, and roles are assigned to users — the same way a job title in a
company bundles a set of responsibilities:

<Mermaid chart={`
flowchart LR
    U["👤 User<br/>(Jane Doe)"] --> R1["🎖️ Role<br/>Sales Manager"]
    U --> R2["🎖️ Role<br/>Employee"]
    R1 --> P1["✔️ view customers"]
    R1 --> P2["✔️ edit customers"]
    R1 --> P3["✔️ export lists"]
    R2 --> P4["✔️ view profile"]
    R2 --> P5["✔️ edit own profile"]
`} />

Jane Doe holds two roles. Everything she can do is the **union** of what those
two roles allow — nothing more, nothing less.

## The three building blocks

| Building block | What it is | Who defines it |
|---|---|---|
| **Permission** | A single allowed action — e.g. *"edit a customer"*, *"view audit history"*. The catalogue is fixed by the product; you cannot invent new permissions. | Primebrick (the system) |
| **Role** | A named bundle of permissions, meaningful in *your* organisation — e.g. *"Sales Manager"*, *"Payroll & HR"*. | Your administrators |
| **Assignment** | The link between a user and a role. One user can hold several roles; one role can be given to many users. | Your administrators |

The key insight: **the system ships the permissions, but your organisation
decides how to group them**. That is what makes the model flexible — it
adapts to how your company is actually organised instead of forcing you
into a fixed hierarchy.

## Shaping roles around your organisation

There is no single "right" way to carve up roles. Two common approaches —
often combined:

**By job title** — one role per position in the org chart:

| Role | Typical permissions |
|---|---|
| Sales Manager | view + edit customers, export lists, view orders |
| Sales Director | everything a Sales Manager can do, plus reports |
| Payroll & HR Manager | view + edit users, manage organisations |
| CFO | read-only access to nearly everything, financial entities in full |

**By company function** — roles mirror departments rather than titles, and
several people share the same role regardless of seniority:

| Role | Typical permissions |
|---|---|
| Sales | customers and orders |
| Operations | configurations and modules |
| Finance & Control | invoices, audit history, read access everywhere |

You can go as fine-grained as you need — a single permission for a
*power-user* role, or broad bundles for *department* roles. The
granularity is a business decision, not a technical constraint.

## Who decides what

<Mermaid chart={`
flowchart TD
    A["Your organisation decides<br/>which roles exist and what<br/>permissions each bundles"] --> B["An administrator creates<br/>the role in Settings"]
    B --> C["The role is assigned<br/>to a user"]
    C --> D["From then on, every screen<br/>and action checks the user's<br/>roles automatically"]
`} />

- **Where roles are created:** in the *Roles* administration screen — see
  [Roles](/docs/user-guide/generated/roles) for the step-by-step procedure.
- **Where assignments happen:** on the user's profile — see
  [Users](/docs/user-guide/generated/users).
- **Where permissions come from:** they are part of the product itself.
  Each area of the application declares what can be done there (view,
  create, edit, delete, restore, audit…). Administrators compose them
  into roles; they never edit the permission list itself.

## A word on administrator roles

Some roles are marked as **administrator roles** — they unlock the
administration areas of the application (user management, role
management, configuration). Assign them deliberately: an administrator
role is not "a role with more permissions", it is the keys to the control
room. Regular users should receive task-oriented roles instead.

## A worked example

> *Jane Doe joins the finance team. The administrator creates her user,
> then assigns two roles: **Finance & Control** (can view every entity,
> read-only) and **Employee** (can manage her own profile). Three months
> later she is promoted to team lead: the administrator adds **Payroll &
> HR Manager**, and — without touching her account in any other way —
> she can now manage users too. Nothing about Jane Doe changed; only the
> bundle of permissions she carries did.*
