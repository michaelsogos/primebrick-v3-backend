# Roles and permissions — how access works

Every screen in Primebrick answers one silent question for you: *"is this
person allowed to do this?"* You never see the question being asked — you
simply notice that a colleague can open a page you cannot, or that a button
is greyed out for some users and not for others. This page explains, in
plain terms, how that decision is made and who controls it.

## The idea in one picture

A user never holds a permission directly. Permissions are bundled into
**roles**, and roles are assigned to users — the same way a job title in a
company bundles a set of responsibilities:

<Mermaid chart={`
flowchart LR
    U["👤 User<br/>(Jane Doe)"] --> R1["🎖️ Role<br/>Sales Manager"]
    U --> R2["🎖️ Role<br/>Employee"]
    R1 --> P1["✔️ view customers"]
    R1 --> P2["✔️ edit customers"]
    R1 --> P3["✔️ export lists"]
    R2 --> P4["✔️ view profile"]
    R2 --> P5["✔️ edit own profile"]
`} />

Jane Doe holds two roles. Everything she can do is the **union** of what those
two roles allow — nothing more, nothing less.

## The three building blocks

| Building block | What it is | Who defines it |
|---|---|---|
| **Permission** | A single allowed action — e.g. *"edit a customer"*, *"view audit history"*. The catalogue is fixed by the product; you cannot invent new permissions. | Primebrick (the system) |
| **Role** | A named bundle of permissions, meaningful in *your* organisation — e.g. *"Sales Manager"*, *"Payroll & HR"*. | Your administrators |
| **Assignment** | The link between a user and a role. One user can hold several roles; one role can be given to many users. | Your administrators |

The key insight: **the system ships the permissions, but your organisation
decides how to group them**. That is what makes the model flexible — it
adapts to how your company is actually organised instead of forcing you
into a fixed hierarchy.

## How a permission is written

Every permission in the catalogue follows one grammar:

```
{scope}.{action}.{cardinality}
```

- **scope** — the entity the permission acts on, in *singular* snake_case:
  `customer`, `organization`, `user_profile`, `role_mapping`,
  `auth_event` (the audit log). Entities that live in a module carry the
  module prefix: `modules.config.read.single`.
- **action** — what is allowed: `read`, `create`, `update`, `delete`,
  `restore`, `duplicate`, plus `audit` (history) and `export`.
- **cardinality** — how much of the scope it covers:
  - `.single` — one record at a time (open a customer, edit a user)
  - `.all` — the whole list (the grid itself)
  - `.bulk` — many records in one shot (bulk delete, bulk restore)
  - read-audit and export have no cardinality: `customer.read.audit`,
    `customer.export`

Real examples from the catalogue:

| Permission | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `customer.read.all` | see the customers list |
| `customer.read.single` | open a single customer |
| `customer.create.single` | create a customer |
| `customer.delete.bulk` | bulk-delete customers |
| `role_mapping.update.single` | edit a role mapping |
| `auth_event.read.audit` | view the audit history |
| `customer.export` | export the customers list |

Every CRUD entity owns the same canonical set of fourteen permissions —
the four read flavours, and the five write actions in both `.single` and
`.bulk` cardinality. You will recognise the pattern everywhere.

### Wildcards

You do not have to list permissions one by one. In a role's permission
list, `*` matches **any sequence of segments**:

| Pattern | Grants |
|---|---|
| `customer.*` | every customer permission — all actions, all cardinalities |
| `customer.read.*` | all three read flavours (`single`, `all`, `audit`) |
| `*.read.all` | list access to every entity |
| `*` | everything — equivalent to an administrator role |

Start broad when a role genuinely owns a whole entity (`finance` gets
`invoice.*`), narrow when it should only look (`auditor` gets
`*.read.single`).

### Where roles come from

A *role mapping* links a **role defined in your identity provider** to the
permission patterns it grants inside Primebrick. When a user signs in,
their identity token carries the IDP roles they hold; the platform looks
each one up in the role mappings table and unfolds the union of the
permission patterns. Nothing is stored on the user record itself —
the mapping *is* the bridge between the outside identity world and the
application's permission catalogue.

## Shaping roles around your organisation

There is no single "right" way to carve up roles. Two common approaches —
often combined:

**By job title** — one role per position in the org chart:

| Role | Typical permissions |
|---|---|
| Sales Manager | view + edit customers, export lists, view orders |
| Sales Director | everything a Sales Manager can do, plus reports |
| Payroll & HR Manager | view + edit users, manage organisations |
| CFO | read-only access to nearly everything, financial entities in full |

**By company function** — roles mirror departments rather than titles, and
several people share the same role regardless of seniority:

| Role | Typical permissions |
|---|---|
| Sales | customers and orders |
| Operations | configurations and modules |
| Finance & Control | invoices, audit history, read access everywhere |

You can go as fine-grained as you need — a single permission for a
*power-user* role, or broad bundles for *department* roles. The
granularity is a business decision, not a technical constraint.

## Who decides what

<Mermaid chart={`
flowchart TD
    A["Your organisation decides<br/>which roles exist and what<br/>permissions each bundles"] --> B["An administrator creates<br/>the role in Settings"]
    B --> C["The role is assigned<br/>to a user"]
    C --> D["From then on, every screen<br/>and action checks the user's<br/>roles automatically"]

- **Where roles are created:** in the *Roles* administration screen — see
  [Roles](/docs/user-guide/generated/roles) for the step-by-step procedure.
- **Where assignments happen:** on the user's profile — see
  [Users](/docs/user-guide/generated/users).
- **Where permissions come from:** they are part of the product itself.
  Each area of the application declares what can be done there (view,
  create, edit, delete, restore, audit…). Administrators compose them
  into roles; they never edit the permission list itself.

## How to grant permissions to a user

1. Open the **Roles** page and create or edit a role mapping: pick the
   **IDP Role**, the **Organization**, a **Label** and the **Permissions**
   patterns to grant (e.g. `customer.*`, `customer.read.single`). See
   [Create a role](/docs/user-guide/generated/roles-create) and
   [Edit a role](/docs/user-guide/generated/roles-edit).
2. Open the user from **Users** and, in the edit form, select the role in
   **Application Roles** — administrator roles show a
   **System Administrator** badge next to their name. See
   [Edit a user](/docs/user-guide/generated/users-edit).
3. Click **Save** — the user now holds the union of all permissions
   granted by the assigned roles.

## A word on administrator roles

Some roles are marked with the **Administrator** flag. This flag does not
merely add permissions — it **bypasses every permission check entirely**:
a user holding an administrator role can perform any action in the
application, equivalent to granting `*`. That is why administrator roles
show the *System Administrator* badge in the Application Roles picker.

Assign them deliberately: an administrator role is not "a role with more
permissions", it is the keys to the control room. Regular users should
receive task-oriented roles instead.

> **Do not confuse the two admin flags.** The *Administrator* flag lives
> on the **role mapping** and grants application administration. The
> separate *Admin / Identity Administrator* flag on the **user record**
> grants administration of the identity-provider system itself — it does
> not make the user an application administrator.

## A worked example

> *Jane Doe joins the finance team. The administrator creates her user,
> then assigns two roles: **Finance & Control** (can view every entity,
> read-only) and **Employee** (can manage her own profile). Three months
> later she is promoted to team lead: the administrator adds **Payroll &
> HR Manager**, and — without touching her account in any other way —
> she can now manage users too. Nothing about Jane Doe changed; only the
> bundle of permissions she carries did.*
